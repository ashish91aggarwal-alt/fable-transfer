Hello, and welcome. Tonight's story is a slow one, and it doesn't need you to follow every word. You can let it drift past you like weather outside a window.

Before we begin, find the position your body likes best. Let your shoulders come down from your ears. Let your hands rest wherever they fall. Take a breath in, a little deeper than usual... and let it go, slowly, the way a tide goes out.

And once more. In... and out.

There's nowhere you need to be now. The day is finished, and whatever it held can wait for the morning. Tonight, we're going somewhere quiet, at the edge of the sea, on a night when the rain has decided to stay.

Our story is called A Rainy Night at the Lighthouse Inn.

The coast path ran along the top of the cliffs like a pale ribbon someone had dropped in the grass. On one side, the land rolled away in fields of heather and gorse, brown and gold and soft green, with stone walls stitching them together. On the other side, far below, lay the sea, grey as a pigeon's wing, breathing in and out against the rocks.

Mara had been walking since the middle of the afternoon. She was a mapmaker, and she had spent the autumn walking this stretch of coast with a notebook in her coat pocket, drawing every cove and stile and crooked signpost, so that one day other walkers could find their way along it too. She liked the work. She liked the slowness of it. You couldn't hurry a map. You had to walk every step of it yourself.

That afternoon, the sky had been wide and pale, with long clouds lying low over the water. But as the light began to fade, the clouds had gathered closer, and the wind had turned, coming in from the sea with a smell of salt and rain in it.

The first drops were small, and soft, and few. One landed on the back of her hand. Another touched her cheek. She pulled up her hood and kept walking, listening to the gentle patter on the fabric above her ears.

The rain grew steadier. It whispered on her hood, and pattered on the flat stones of the path, and somewhere far below, it hushed into the sea. It darkened the stones of the path and made the grass shine. It gathered on the heather in tiny beads. It turned the sea from grey to silver, and then to a deep, quiet blue, as the evening came down over everything.

Mara wasn't worried. She knew where she was going. A little further along the path, on the next headland, there was an inn. She had heard about it from a farmer two villages back, who had leaned on his gate and told her, in no hurry at all, that if she kept to the cliff path she would come to the old lighthouse at Saltmere Head, and beside it, the keeper's cottage, which had been an inn for many years now. Warm beds, he had said. Good soup. And a fire that never seemed to go out.

So she walked on, through the soft rain and the gathering dark, and she watched for the light.

The path dipped down into a little valley, where a stream ran under a wooden footbridge. She paused there for a moment, resting her hands on the damp rail, listening to the water chattering over the stones below. The rain made small rings on the surface of the stream, and the rings spread and met and faded, over and over, never quite the same twice.

Then she climbed the other side, step by step, her boots finding the worn places where other walkers had gone before her. At the top, the land opened out again, and the wind came to meet her, cool and clean.

And there it was.

Out on the very end of the headland, a white tower stood against the darkening sky. At the top of it, behind a band of glass, a great light was turning. Slowly, slowly, a long pale beam swept out across the water, reached the far edge of the sky, and swung around again. It swept over the sea, and over the rocks, and for a moment it passed over the fields too, and over Mara herself, standing on the path in the rain. Then it moved on, patient and unhurried, and the dark came softly back.

She stood and watched it go around once. And again. And again. There was something very calming about it, the way it never rushed and never stopped. As if it had all the time in the world, and was happy to share it.

Below the tower, tucked into the shelter of a low stone wall, was a long, white cottage with a slate roof. Its windows glowed yellow, every one of them, warm squares of light in the blue evening. Smoke rose from its chimney and leaned away on the wind. And above the door, a wooden sign swung gently on its chains. Even from the path she could read the painted letters. The Lighthouse Inn.

Mara smiled to herself, and followed the path down.

It was a short walk, but she took it slowly, because she wanted to remember it. The crunch of gravel under her boots as the path became a little lane. The smell of woodsmoke mixing with the salt air. The sound of the sea growing softer as the wall rose up beside her and took the edge off the wind. A garden gate, painted blue, that squeaked a little as she opened it, and swung shut behind her with a gentle click.

There was a porch over the front door, with a bench inside it and a row of hooks for wet coats. A pair of old boots stood by the step, filled with geraniums that had kept on flowering into the autumn, red and stubborn. A brass bell hung beside the door on a curl of iron.

Mara shook the rain from her hood, and rang it gently. A small, soft note, that faded into the sound of the rain.

Footsteps inside. The quiet lift of a latch. And the door opened onto warmth, and light, and the smell of bread.
