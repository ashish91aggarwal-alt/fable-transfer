When they came back down the steps, around and around and around, the inn was quieter. The old man had folded away his net and gone up to bed. The woman in the green cardigan had wound her wool into a neat ball and tucked her needles into it. The fire had burned down to a deep red glow, and Biscuit had stretched himself out full length in front of it, like a long grey cushion, fast asleep.

Tom said goodnight, and went back out into the soft dark, to his little room at the bottom of the tower.

Mara sat down for a moment in the armchair by the embers. She took her notebook from her coat pocket, and opened it to the page for that day, beside her little drawing of the headland. She looked up at the old map by the fireplace, at its faded blue star. Then, very carefully, she drew a tiny blue star of her own, where the lighthouse stood. And underneath it, in small neat letters, she wrote: Lighthouse Inn. Warm beds. Good soup. A fire that never seems to go out.

She closed the notebook. Mrs Penrose was waiting at the foot of the stairs with a candle in a little brass holder, and a hot water bottle in a knitted cover, the colour of oatmeal.

"You're right at the top," she said. "Under the roof. You'll hear the rain up there, if it comes back. Most people find it helps."

The stairs creaked softly under Mara's feet, each one with its own small voice. Her room was small and low, tucked in under the slope of the roof, with a dark beam along the ceiling and a little window at the far end. And under the slope, there was the bed. A deep, soft bed, with a white iron frame, and a patchwork quilt in faded blues and creams, and two plump pillows. The corner of the covers was already turned down, neat and white and waiting.

She changed into her nightclothes, and folded her walking clothes over the chair for the morning. Then she climbed into bed.

The sheets were cool at first, and then quickly warm. She slid the hot water bottle down to her feet, still in their borrowed woollen socks, and the warmth of it spread slowly up through her, into her ankles, and her knees, and all the way up. She pulled the quilt up to her chin. The pillow was soft, and gave beneath her head, and held it.

She blew out the candle.

For a moment the room was very dark. And then, softly, a pale light came drifting across the ceiling, from the little window. It moved slowly over the beam, and the sloping roof, and faded away. A little while later, it came again. And then again.

The lighthouse. Going round. Keeping its patient watch over the sea, and the boats, and the little inn, and her.

Outside, the rain came back. Not hard. Just softly, the way it had been all evening. A gentle patter on the slates above her head. A few drops at first, then more, then a soft and steady rhythm, like fingertips on a tabletop, very lightly, very slowly. It ran along the gutter and trickled down the pipe outside the window.

Somewhere downstairs, the last log settled into the embers.

Mara's eyes were heavy now. She let them close.

She listened to the rain on the roof.

She listened to the far, soft hush of the sea, breathing in, and breathing out.

She felt the warmth at her feet, and the weight of the quilt.

She thought about the little footbridge over the stream, where the raindrops made rings on the water. The rings spreading out, and meeting, and fading.

Spreading out. And meeting. And fading.

She thought about the steps, going around and around.

She thought about the boats far out on the dark water, moving slowly home.

And the light came around again, across the ceiling. Slow, and soft, and pale. And faded. And came again.

The map could wait for the morning. The path would still be there.

For now, there was only the rain. And the sea. And the light, going round.

Patter, patter, on the roof.

Breathing in. And out.

The light, going round.

Warm. And still.

The light, going round.

And round.

And round.

Sleep well.
