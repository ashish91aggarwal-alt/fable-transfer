When they came back down the hundred and twelve steps, around and around and around, the inn was quieter. The old man had folded away his net and gone up to bed. The woman in the green cardigan had wound her wool into a neat ball and tucked her needles into it. The fire had burned down to a deep red glow, and Biscuit had stretched himself out full length in front of it, like a long grey cushion, fast asleep.

Mrs Penrose was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with a candle in a little brass holder, and a hot water bottle in a knitted cover, the colour of oatmeal.

"Your room's at the top," she said. "Under the roof. You'll hear the rain up there, if it comes back. Most people find it helps."

Mara thanked Tom, and said goodnight. He nodded, and smiled, and went back out into the soft dark, to his little room at the bottom of the tower. Mara watched through the window as his lantern bobbed across the yard, and the tower door opened, and closed.

Then she took the candle, and the hot water bottle, and climbed the stairs.

The stairs in the inn were wooden, and they creaked gently under her feet, each one with its own small voice. The candle made her shadow tall on the wall beside her, and then short, and then tall again as she turned the corner. At the top, there was a little landing with a round window, and a door with a china handle painted with tiny blue flowers.

Her room was small and low and cosy, tucked in under the slope of the roof. The ceiling came down on one side, almost to the floor, with a dark wooden beam running along it. There was a washstand with a jug and bowl, and a chest of drawers with a lace mat on top, and a chair with a cushion. And under the slope of the roof, there was the bed.

It was a deep, soft bed, with an iron frame painted white, and a patchwork quilt in faded blues and creams and roses. There were two plump pillows, and a woollen blanket folded across the foot. Mrs Penrose had already turned down the corner of the covers, so that the white sheet showed, neat and inviting.

Mara set the candle on the little table by the bed. She changed into her nightclothes slowly, folding her walking clothes over the back of the chair for the morning. She washed her face in the bowl, and the water was cool and soft and smelled faintly of lavender. She brushed out her hair. She took her notebook from her coat pocket, and opened it, and on the page for that day, beside the little drawing of the headland, she wrote, in small neat letters: Lighthouse Inn. Warm beds. Good soup. A fire that never goes out.

Then she closed the notebook, and set it beside the candle, and climbed into bed.

The sheets were cool at first, and then quickly warm. She slid the hot water bottle down to her feet, still in their borrowed woollen socks, and the warmth of it spread slowly up through her, into her ankles, and her knees, and all the way up. She pulled the quilt up to her chin. The pillow was soft, and gave beneath her head, and held it.

She blew out the candle.

For a moment the room was very dark. And then, softly, a pale light came drifting across the ceiling, from the small window at the end of the room. It moved slowly over the beam, and the sloping roof, and the top of the wall, and faded away. A little while later, it came again. And then again.

The lighthouse. Going round. Keeping its patient watch over the sea, and the boats, and the sleeping village, and the little inn, and her.

Outside, the rain came back. Not hard. Just softly, the way it had been all evening. A gentle patter on the slates above her head. A few drops at first, then more, then a soft and steady rhythm, like fingertips on a tabletop, very lightly, very slowly. It ran along the gutters and trickled down the pipe outside the window. It was the most comfortable sound in the world, to be warm and dry, and listening to the rain.

Somewhere downstairs, the last log in the fireplace settled into the embers with a sigh.

Mara's eyes were heavy now. She let them close. She listened to the rain on the roof. Patter, patter, patter. She listened to the far, soft hush of the sea at the bottom of the cliffs, breathing in, and breathing out. She felt the warmth of the hot water bottle at her feet, and the weight of the quilt, and the softness of the pillow.

She thought about the path she had walked that day. The heather, and the gorse, and the little footbridge over the stream, where the raindrops made rings on the water. The rings spreading out, and meeting, and fading. Spreading out, and meeting, and fading.

She thought about the hundred and twelve steps, going around and around and around.

She thought about the great glass lens, turning slowly, and the little rainbows drifting over her hands.

She thought about the boats far out on the dark water, red and green and white, moving slowly home.

And the light came around again, across the ceiling. Slow, and soft, and pale. And faded. And came again.

There was nowhere she needed to be. Nothing she needed to do. The map could wait for the morning. The path would still be there. The sea would still be there, calm and bright, and the boats would go out early, and Mrs Penrose would have breakfast on the table, and Biscuit would be back by the fire.

But that was tomorrow.

For now, there was only the rain. And the sea. And the light, going round.

Patter, patter, on the roof.

Breathing in. And out.

The light, going round.

Warm. And safe. And still.

The light, going round.

And round.

And round.

Sleep well.
