Hello, and welcome. Tonight's story is a slow one, and it doesn't need you to follow every word. You can let it drift past you like weather outside a window.

Before we begin, find the position your body likes best. Let your shoulders come down from your ears. Let your hands rest wherever they fall. Take a breath in, a little deeper than usual... and let it go, slowly, the way a tide goes out.

And once more. In... and out.

Good. There's nowhere you need to be now. The day is finished, and whatever it held can wait for the morning. Tonight, we're going somewhere quiet, at the edge of the sea, on a night when the rain has decided to stay.

Our story is called A Rainy Night at the Lighthouse Inn.

The coast path ran along the top of the cliffs like a pale ribbon someone had dropped in the grass. On one side, the land rolled away in fields of heather and gorse, brown and gold and soft green, with stone walls stitching them together. On the other side, far below, lay the sea, grey as a pigeon's wing, breathing in and out against the rocks.

Mara had been walking since the middle of the afternoon. She was a mapmaker, and she had spent the autumn walking this stretch of coast with a notebook in her coat pocket, drawing every cove and stile and crooked signpost, so that one day other walkers could find their way along it too. She liked the work. She liked the slowness of it. You couldn't hurry a map. You had to walk every step of it yourself.

That afternoon, the sky had been wide and pale, with long clouds lying low over the water. But as the light began to fade, the clouds had gathered closer, and the wind had turned, coming in from the sea with a smell of salt and rain in it.

The first drops were small, and soft, and few. One landed on the back of her hand. Another touched her cheek. She pulled up her hood and kept walking, listening to the gentle patter on the fabric above her ears.

The rain grew steadier. Not hard, not cold, just steady, the way rain is when it has made up its mind. It darkened the stones of the path and made the grass shine. It gathered on the heather in tiny beads. It turned the sea from grey to silver, and then to a deep, quiet blue, as the evening came down over everything.

Mara wasn't worried. She knew where she was going. A little further along the path, on the next headland, there was an inn. She had heard about it from a farmer two villages back, who had leaned on his gate and told her, in no hurry at all, that if she kept to the cliff path she would come to the old lighthouse at Saltmere Head, and beside it, the keeper's cottage, which had been an inn for many years now. Warm beds, he had said. Good soup. And a fire that never seemed to go out.

So she walked on, through the soft rain and the gathering dark, and she watched for the light.

The path dipped down into a little valley, where a stream ran under a wooden footbridge. She paused there for a moment, resting her hands on the damp rail, listening to the water chattering over the stones below. The rain made small rings on the surface of the stream, and the rings spread and met and faded, over and over, never quite the same twice.

Then she climbed the other side, step by step, her boots finding the worn places where other walkers had gone before her. At the top, the land opened out again, and the wind came to meet her, cool and clean.

And there it was.

Out on the very end of the headland, a white tower stood against the darkening sky. At the top of it, behind a band of glass, a great light was turning. Slowly, slowly, a long pale beam swept out across the water, reached the far edge of the sky, and swung around again. It swept over the sea, and over the rocks, and for a moment it passed over the fields too, and over Mara herself, standing on the path in the rain. Then it moved on, patient and unhurried, and the dark came softly back.

She stood and watched it go around once. And again. And again. There was something very calming about it, the way it never rushed and never stopped. As if it had all the time in the world, and was happy to share it.

Below the tower, tucked into the shelter of a low stone wall, was a long, white cottage with a slate roof. Its windows glowed yellow, every one of them, warm squares of light in the blue evening. Smoke rose from its chimney and leaned away on the wind. And above the door, a wooden sign swung gently on its chains. Even from the path she could read the painted letters. The Lighthouse Inn.

Mara smiled to herself, and followed the path down.

It was a short walk, but she took it slowly, because she wanted to remember it. The crunch of gravel under her boots as the path became a little lane. The smell of woodsmoke mixing with the salt air. The sound of the sea growing softer as the wall rose up beside her and took the edge off the wind. A garden gate, painted blue, that squeaked a little as she opened it, and swung shut behind her with a gentle click.

There was a porch over the front door, with a bench inside it and a row of hooks for wet coats. A pair of old boots stood by the step, filled with geraniums that had kept on flowering into the autumn, red and stubborn. A brass bell hung beside the door on a curl of iron.

Mara shook the rain from her hood, and rang it. One clear note, that seemed to hang in the damp air for a long time.

Footsteps inside. A bolt drawn back. And the door opened onto warmth, and light, and the smell of bread.
The woman in the doorway was small and round, with grey hair pinned up in a soft knot and a flowery apron tied over a dark blue jumper. Her cheeks were pink from the kitchen. She looked at Mara, and at the rain behind her, and her face crinkled into a smile.

"Well now," she said. "You've walked a long way, by the look of you. Come in, come in, before the weather follows you."

Her name was Mrs Penrose, and she had kept the inn for longer than she could easily count. She took Mara's wet coat and hung it on a hook by the door, where it dripped onto a rag rug that had clearly caught a great many wet coats before. She looked at Mara's boots, and then she fetched a pair of thick wool socks from a basket under the stairs, the way someone might fetch a cup of tea, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

"Everyone needs dry feet," she said. "Put those on, and come through to the fire."

Mara sat on the bottom step of the stairs and unlaced her boots, slowly, one lace and then the other. Her feet were cold and a little damp. The socks were soft, and slightly too big, and warm from sitting near the kitchen. She pulled them on, and wriggled her toes, and felt the day's walking begin to leave her, one small piece at a time.

The front room of the inn was long and low, with dark beams across the ceiling and a floor of old flagstones, smoothed by many years of feet. At one end stood a wide stone fireplace, and in it, a fire was burning, steady and golden, the logs settled into a bed of glowing embers. Every so often a log shifted and sighed, and a little shower of sparks went up the chimney.

Around the fire there were armchairs, deep ones, with faded cushions and woollen blankets folded over their arms. There were small tables with lamps on them, and on the windowsills, candles in jars, and shells, and pieces of sea glass in blue and green, lined up like tiny treasures. Along one wall, shelves were filled with books, their spines worn and soft. And on the wall above the fireplace hung an old painting of the lighthouse itself, white against a stormy sky, its beam reaching out over the waves.

The windows were small and deep set, with wide sills you could sit on. The rain ran down the glass in slow, wandering lines, catching the firelight and turning it to gold. Every few moments, the beam from the lighthouse passed outside, and the rain on the windows lit up all at once, silver, and then faded again.

There were only a few other guests that evening. In a chair near the window, an old man with a white beard sat mending a fishing net spread over his knees. His hands moved slowly and surely, threading the twine, tying small neat knots, pulling them snug. He nodded to Mara when she came in, the kind of nod that says good evening without needing to say anything at all, and went back to his net.

On the far side of the fire, a woman in a green cardigan was knitting something long and soft in the colour of the sea. Her needles clicked gently, a quiet, regular rhythm, like a clock that wasn't in any hurry. Now and then she paused to count her stitches under her breath, and then the clicking began again.

And curled up on the hearthrug, as close to the fire as it was possible to be without actually being in it, lay a large grey cat. Its fur was soft and thick, the colour of rain clouds, and its paws were tucked neatly underneath it. As Mara sat down in the armchair nearest the fire, the cat opened one golden eye, considered her for a long moment, and closed it again. A slow, deep purr began somewhere inside it, like a very small engine idling.

"That's Biscuit," said Mrs Penrose, coming in with a tray. "He thinks the fire belongs to him. We let him go on thinking it."

On the tray there was a bowl of soup, a thick slice of bread, and a little dish of butter. The soup was made of leeks and potatoes, pale and creamy, with a swirl of cream on top and a scattering of pepper. The bread was still warm, with a dark crust that crackled when she broke it, and a soft white middle. Mara spread the butter on it and watched it melt.

She ate slowly. There was no reason to hurry. The soup was warm all the way down, and the bread was the kind of bread that makes you close your eyes for a moment when you taste it. Outside, the rain kept up its gentle drumming on the roof. Inside, the fire crackled, and the needles clicked, and the cat purred, and the old man tied his knots, one after another.

When she had finished, Mrs Penrose took the tray away and came back with a mug of hot cocoa, and set it on the little table at Mara's elbow. Then she sat down in the chair across from her, with a sigh of someone who had been on her feet all day and was glad to be off them.

"So," she said. "What brings you all the way out to Saltmere Head, in weather like this?"

Mara told her about the maps. About walking the coast path from village to village, drawing every bend and every bench, every spring where you could fill a water bottle, every place where the view opened up and made you stop. Mrs Penrose listened, nodding, her hands folded in her lap.

"Then you'll want to know about the lighthouse," she said, when Mara had finished. "Every good map has a story or two on it."

And she told her.

The lighthouse had stood on the headland for nearly two hundred years. For most of that time, there had been a keeper living in the cottage, climbing the tower every evening to light the lamp, and every morning to put it out. They had polished the great glass lens until it shone, and trimmed the wicks, and wound the clockwork that kept the light turning, all through the long nights, winter and summer. The keepers' families had lived here too. Children had grown up in these rooms, and played on these flagstones, and fallen asleep to the sound of the sea.

These days, the light mostly looked after itself. But there was still a keeper of sorts, a man called Tom, who lived in the little room at the bottom of the tower and made sure the old light kept its patient watch. He came over to the inn most evenings for his supper, Mrs Penrose said, and he would probably be along soon. And if Mara asked him nicely, he might take her up to see the lamp.

Mara wrapped both hands around her mug of cocoa, and felt the warmth of it soak into her fingers. She looked into the fire. She watched the flames curl around the logs, blue at the bottom, orange in the middle, and yellow at the tips, always moving, never the same shape twice. The heat of it rested on her face and on her knees like a blanket.

She thought she might like to see the lamp very much.

The old man by the window had finished one corner of his net, and moved on to the next. The woman in the green cardigan had turned her row, and started back along the other way. Biscuit stretched one front paw out towards the fire, spread his toes, and drew it slowly back in again. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, softly, softly.

And outside, the light went round. And round. And round.
After a while, the front door opened, letting in a breath of cool, rain-scented air, and a tall man ducked in under the low beam. He wore a heavy oilskin coat, shining wet, and a knitted hat pulled down over his ears. He hung up the coat, and pulled off the hat, and his hair stood up in a grey tuft at the back. He had the slow, easy movements of someone who has spent a lot of time on ladders and stairs, and learned not to hurry on them.

This was Tom.

He sat at the table by the window and ate his supper, the same soup and bread that Mara had eaten, and talked quietly with the old man about the weather and the boats. The rain would ease by morning, they agreed. It usually did, this time of year. The sea would be calm and bright, and the boats would go out early.

When he had finished, Mrs Penrose leaned over and said something to him, and he looked across at Mara, and smiled.

"You'd like to see the light?" he said.

She said that she would.

"Then you'd better bring your coat," he said. "It's a fair few steps up. But it's worth it, on a night like this."

They went out together, across the little yard between the cottage and the tower. The rain had softened to a fine, drifting mist that settled on Mara's face and hair like the lightest touch. The air smelled of the sea and wet stone. Above them, the tower rose up white into the dark, and at its top, the beam swung slowly out across the water, and away, and back again.

Tom unlocked a heavy wooden door at the bottom of the tower, and they stepped inside.

It was quiet in there. Very quiet. The thick stone walls held the sound of the wind and the sea far away, so that all Mara could hear was a soft dripping somewhere, and her own breath, and the faint tick of something mechanical far above. There was a small room with a stove, and a chair, and a kettle, and a shelf of logbooks with dates written on their spines. And in the middle of the room, a stone staircase began, curling up and around the inside of the tower, around and around, out of sight.

"A hundred and twelve steps," said Tom. "I count them every night. Some nights I lose count, and have to start again."

They began to climb.

The steps were worn into soft hollows in the middle, smoothed by two hundred years of keepers' boots. A brass handrail ran up beside them, cool under Mara's hand. Every so often there was a small round window set deep in the wall, and through each one, she could see a little more of the night outside. First the roof of the cottage, its chimney still smoking. Then the garden wall, and the lane. Then the dark fields, and the cliff edge, and the sea beyond, wide and black and gently moving.

Step after step. Around and around. Mara counted them, the way Tom did. Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. The rhythm of it was soothing, the same small movement, again and again, up and around, up and around. Forty. Forty-one. Her legs were tired from the day's walking, but it was a pleasant tiredness, the kind that tells you that you'll sleep well tonight. Sixty. Sixty-one. The ticking above them grew a little louder. Eighty. Eighty-one. She lost count somewhere after ninety, and didn't mind.

And then they came up through a hatch in the floor, into the lantern room at the very top of the tower.

It was like stepping inside a jewel.

All around them, the walls were made of glass, tall panes of it, beaded with rain. In the centre of the room stood the great lens, taller than Tom, made of hundreds of curved glass prisms, stacked in rings, one above the other, like the layers of a huge glass beehive. In the heart of it, the lamp was glowing, and the lens gathered up its light and bent it and shaped it, into that long, steady beam. The whole thing was turning, slowly, on its base, with a soft whirring sound, as smooth as honey.

As it turned, the light moved through the prisms, and they glittered. Small rainbows slid across the walls, and the floor, and the backs of Mara's hands. Red, and orange, and gold, and green, and the softest violet. They drifted over her, and moved on, and others came in their place.

Tom showed her how the lamp worked. Not quickly, and not all at once, but bit by bit, the way you might show a friend around your garden. He showed her where the old keepers had wound the clockwork by hand, a great brass handle that they had turned every few hours, all night long. He showed her the little brushes and cloths they had used to polish the glass, still hanging neatly on their hooks. He showed her the logbook, open on a small wooden desk, where he wrote down the weather every night in neat, small handwriting.

Wind, south-westerly, light, he had written. Rain, steady. Sea, calm.

Then he opened a narrow door in the glass, and they stepped out onto the gallery, the narrow iron balcony that ran all the way around the outside of the lantern.

The air was cool and damp and very fresh. The rain had almost stopped now, only a few soft drops drifting on the breeze. Mara held onto the rail and looked out.

The whole world lay below her, quiet and dark. The sea stretched away to the horizon, so wide and so calm that it seemed to breathe. Far out on it, she could see the tiny lights of boats, a red one, and a green one, and a few white ones, moving very slowly, like stars that had come down to rest on the water. Along the coast, there were the lights of the villages she had walked through, small golden clusters tucked into the folds of the cliffs. And above it all, the clouds were beginning to thin, and here and there, between them, a few real stars were showing, faint and far away.

And behind her, the great light turned.

She felt it more than saw it, at first. A soft brightness that came around behind her, and passed over her shoulder, and reached out across the sea. She watched the beam travel over the water, long and pale and slow, lighting up the tops of the gentle waves, and the mist, and the distant boats. It reached the edge of the world, and kept going, around, and around, until it came back to her again.

"People ask me if I get tired of it," said Tom quietly, beside her. "Watching it go round, night after night. But I never do. It's like listening to someone breathing, when they're asleep. It tells you everything is all right."

Mara watched the light go round. Once. And again. And again.

The sea breathed in, and out. The light swept out, and back. The boats moved slowly on the dark water. The stars came out, one by one. And everything, everywhere, was all right.

They stood there for a long time, not talking. There wasn't any need to.
When they came back down the hundred and twelve steps, around and around and around, the inn was quieter. The old man had folded away his net and gone up to bed. The woman in the green cardigan had wound her wool into a neat ball and tucked her needles into it. The fire had burned down to a deep red glow, and Biscuit had stretched himself out full length in front of it, like a long grey cushion, fast asleep.

Mrs Penrose was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with a candle in a little brass holder, and a hot water bottle in a knitted cover, the colour of oatmeal.

"Your room's at the top," she said. "Under the roof. You'll hear the rain up there, if it comes back. Most people find it helps."

Mara thanked Tom, and said goodnight. He nodded, and smiled, and went back out into the soft dark, to his little room at the bottom of the tower. Mara watched through the window as his lantern bobbed across the yard, and the tower door opened, and closed.

Then she took the candle, and the hot water bottle, and climbed the stairs.

The stairs in the inn were wooden, and they creaked gently under her feet, each one with its own small voice. The candle made her shadow tall on the wall beside her, and then short, and then tall again as she turned the corner. At the top, there was a little landing with a round window, and a door with a china handle painted with tiny blue flowers.

Her room was small and low and cosy, tucked in under the slope of the roof. The ceiling came down on one side, almost to the floor, with a dark wooden beam running along it. There was a washstand with a jug and bowl, and a chest of drawers with a lace mat on top, and a chair with a cushion. And under the slope of the roof, there was the bed.

It was a deep, soft bed, with an iron frame painted white, and a patchwork quilt in faded blues and creams and roses. There were two plump pillows, and a woollen blanket folded across the foot. Mrs Penrose had already turned down the corner of the covers, so that the white sheet showed, neat and inviting.

Mara set the candle on the little table by the bed. She changed into her nightclothes slowly, folding her walking clothes over the back of the chair for the morning. She washed her face in the bowl, and the water was cool and soft and smelled faintly of lavender. She brushed out her hair. She took her notebook from her coat pocket, and opened it, and on the page for that day, beside the little drawing of the headland, she wrote, in small neat letters: Lighthouse Inn. Warm beds. Good soup. A fire that never goes out.

Then she closed the notebook, and set it beside the candle, and climbed into bed.

The sheets were cool at first, and then quickly warm. She slid the hot water bottle down to her feet, still in their borrowed woollen socks, and the warmth of it spread slowly up through her, into her ankles, and her knees, and all the way up. She pulled the quilt up to her chin. The pillow was soft, and gave beneath her head, and held it.

She blew out the candle.

For a moment the room was very dark. And then, softly, a pale light came drifting across the ceiling, from the small window at the end of the room. It moved slowly over the beam, and the sloping roof, and the top of the wall, and faded away. A little while later, it came again. And then again.

The lighthouse. Going round. Keeping its patient watch over the sea, and the boats, and the sleeping village, and the little inn, and her.

Outside, the rain came back. Not hard. Just softly, the way it had been all evening. A gentle patter on the slates above her head. A few drops at first, then more, then a soft and steady rhythm, like fingertips on a tabletop, very lightly, very slowly. It ran along the gutters and trickled down the pipe outside the window. It was the most comfortable sound in the world, to be warm and dry, and listening to the rain.

Somewhere downstairs, the last log in the fireplace settled into the embers with a sigh.

Mara's eyes were heavy now. She let them close. She listened to the rain on the roof. Patter, patter, patter. She listened to the far, soft hush of the sea at the bottom of the cliffs, breathing in, and breathing out. She felt the warmth of the hot water bottle at her feet, and the weight of the quilt, and the softness of the pillow.

She thought about the path she had walked that day. The heather, and the gorse, and the little footbridge over the stream, where the raindrops made rings on the water. The rings spreading out, and meeting, and fading. Spreading out, and meeting, and fading.

She thought about the hundred and twelve steps, going around and around and around.

She thought about the great glass lens, turning slowly, and the little rainbows drifting over her hands.

She thought about the boats far out on the dark water, red and green and white, moving slowly home.

And the light came around again, across the ceiling. Slow, and soft, and pale. And faded. And came again.

There was nowhere she needed to be. Nothing she needed to do. The map could wait for the morning. The path would still be there. The sea would still be there, calm and bright, and the boats would go out early, and Mrs Penrose would have breakfast on the table, and Biscuit would be back by the fire.

But that was tomorrow.

For now, there was only the rain. And the sea. And the light, going round.

Patter, patter, on the roof.

Breathing in. And out.

The light, going round.

Warm. And safe. And still.

The light, going round.

And round.

And round.

Sleep well.
