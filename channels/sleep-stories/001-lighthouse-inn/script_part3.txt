After a while, the front door opened, letting in a breath of cool, rain-scented air, and a tall man ducked in under the low beam. He wore a heavy oilskin coat, shining wet, and a knitted hat pulled down over his ears. He hung up the coat, and pulled off the hat, and his hair stood up in a grey tuft at the back. He had the slow, easy movements of someone who has spent a lot of time on ladders and stairs, and learned not to hurry on them.

This was Tom.

He sat at the table by the window and ate his supper, the same soup and bread that Mara had eaten, and talked quietly with the old man about the weather and the boats. The rain would ease by morning, they agreed. It usually did, this time of year. The sea would be calm and bright, and the boats would go out early.

When he had finished, Mrs Penrose leaned over and said something to him, and he looked across at Mara, and smiled.

"You'd like to see the light?" he said.

She said that she would.

"Then you'd better bring your coat," he said. "It's a fair few steps up. But it's worth it, on a night like this."

They went out together, across the little yard between the cottage and the tower. The rain had softened to a fine, drifting mist that settled on Mara's face and hair like the lightest touch. The air smelled of the sea and wet stone. Above them, the tower rose up white into the dark, and at its top, the beam swung slowly out across the water, and away, and back again.

Tom unlocked a heavy wooden door at the bottom of the tower, and they stepped inside.

It was quiet in there. Very quiet. The thick stone walls held the sound of the wind and the sea far away, so that all Mara could hear was a soft dripping somewhere, and her own breath, and the faint tick of something mechanical far above. There was a small room with a stove, and a chair, and a kettle, and a shelf of logbooks with dates written on their spines. And in the middle of the room, a stone staircase began, curling up and around the inside of the tower, around and around, out of sight.

"It's a good few steps," said Tom. "I used to count them. These days I just let my feet remember the way."

They began to climb.

The steps were worn into soft hollows in the middle, smoothed by two hundred years of keepers' boots. A brass handrail ran up beside them, cool under Mara's hand. Every so often there was a small round window set deep in the wall, and through each one, she could see a little more of the night outside. First the roof of the cottage, its chimney still smoking. Then the garden wall, and the lane. Then the dark fields, with one or two farm lights glowing in them. And beyond them, the sea, gently moving.

Step after step. Around and around. Mara didn't count them. She let her feet find the rhythm, the same small movement, again and again, up and around, up and around. Her legs were tired from the day's walking, but it was a pleasant tiredness, the kind that tells you that you'll sleep well tonight. Up and around. The ticking above them grew a little softer, and a little closer. Up and around. Up and around.

And then they came up through a hatch in the floor, into the lantern room at the very top of the tower.

The light was everywhere at once, soft and moving. A small patch of it slid across Mara's sleeve, and away.

All around them, the walls were made of glass, tall panes of it, beaded with rain. In the centre of the room stood the great lens, taller than Tom, made of hundreds of curved glass prisms, stacked in rings, one above the other, like the layers of a huge glass beehive. In the heart of it, the lamp was glowing, and the lens gathered up its light and bent it and shaped it, into that long, steady beam. The whole thing was turning, slowly, on its base, with a soft whirring sound, as smooth as honey.

As it turned, the light moved through the prisms, and they glittered. Small rainbows slid across the walls, and the floor, and the backs of Mara's hands. Red, and orange, and gold, and green, and the softest violet. They drifted over her, and moved on, and others came in their place.

Tom showed her how the lamp worked. Not quickly, and not all at once, but bit by bit, the way you might show a friend around your garden. He showed her where the old keepers had wound the clockwork by hand, a great brass handle that they had turned every few hours, all night long. He showed her the little brushes and cloths they had used to polish the glass, still hanging neatly on their hooks. He showed her the logbook, open on a small wooden desk, where he wrote down the weather every night in neat, small handwriting.

Wind, south-westerly, light, he had written. Rain, steady. Sea, calm.

Then he came and stood beside her at the glass, and they looked out together.

The rain had almost stopped now. Only a few soft drops still clung to the panes, and slid slowly down, and joined together, and slid on.

Beyond the glass, the whole world lay quiet and dark. The sea stretched away to the horizon, so wide and so calm that it seemed to breathe. Far out on it, Mara could see the tiny lights of boats, a red one, and a green one, and a few white ones, moving very slowly, like stars that had come down to rest on the water. Along the coast, there were the lights of the villages she had walked through, small golden clusters tucked into the folds of the land. And above it all, the clouds were beginning to thin, and here and there, between them, a few real stars were showing, faint and far away.

And beside them, the great lens turned.

Its beam passed out through the glass, long and pale and slow, and travelled over the water, lighting the tops of the gentle waves, and the mist, and the distant boats. It reached the edge of the world, and kept going, around, and around, until it came back again.

"People ask me if I get tired of it," said Tom quietly. "Watching it go round, night after night. But I never do. It's like listening to someone breathing, when they're asleep."

Mara watched the light go round. Once. And again. And again.

The sea breathed in, and out. The light swept out, and back. The boats moved slowly on the dark water. The stars came out, one by one.

They stood there for a long time, not talking. There wasn't any need to.
