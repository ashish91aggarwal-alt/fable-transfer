The woman in the doorway was small and round, with grey hair pinned up in a soft knot and a flowery apron tied over a dark blue jumper. Her cheeks were pink from the kitchen. She looked at Mara, and at the rain behind her, and her face crinkled into a smile.

"Well now," she said. "You've walked a long way, by the look of you. Come in, come in, before the weather follows you."

Her name was Mrs Penrose, and she had kept the inn for longer than she could easily count. She took Mara's wet coat and hung it on a hook by the door, where it dripped onto a rag rug that had clearly caught a great many wet coats before. She looked at Mara's boots, and then she fetched a pair of thick wool socks from a basket under the stairs, the way someone might fetch a cup of tea, as if it were the most natural thing in the world.

"Everyone needs dry feet," she said. "Put those on, and come through to the fire."

Mara sat on the bottom step of the stairs and unlaced her boots, slowly, one lace and then the other. Her feet were cold and a little damp. The socks were soft, and slightly too big, and warm from sitting near the kitchen. She pulled them on, and wriggled her toes, and felt the day's walking begin to leave her, one small piece at a time.

The front room of the inn was long and low, with dark beams across the ceiling and a floor of old flagstones, smoothed by many years of feet. At one end stood a wide stone fireplace, and in it, a fire was burning, steady and golden, the logs settled into a bed of glowing embers. Every so often a log shifted and sighed, and a little shower of sparks went up the chimney.

Around the fire there were armchairs, deep ones, with faded cushions and woollen blankets folded over their arms. There were small tables with lamps on them, and on the windowsills, candles in jars, and shells, and pieces of sea glass in blue and green, lined up like tiny treasures. Along one wall, shelves were filled with books, their spines worn and soft. And on the wall above the fireplace hung an old painting of the lighthouse itself, white against a grey evening sky, its beam reaching out over the water.

Beside the fireplace, in a plain wooden frame, there was something that made Mara look twice. An old map of the headland, drawn by hand, long ago. The cliffs were traced in fine brown lines, as careful as the veins of a leaf. The sea was washed in pale blue. And where the lighthouse stood, someone had marked it with a tiny five-pointed star, in faded blue ink.

The windows were small and deep set, with wide sills you could sit on. The rain ran down the glass in slow, wandering lines, catching the firelight and turning it to gold. Every few moments, the beam from the lighthouse passed outside, and the rain on the windows lit up all at once, silver, and then faded again.

There were only a few other guests that evening. In a chair near the window, an old man with a white beard sat mending a fishing net spread over his knees. His hands moved slowly and surely, threading the twine, tying small neat knots, pulling them snug. He nodded to Mara when she came in, the kind of nod that says good evening without needing to say anything at all, and went back to his net.

On the far side of the fire, a woman in a green cardigan was knitting something long and soft in the colour of the sea. Her needles clicked gently, a quiet, regular rhythm, like a clock that wasn't in any hurry. Now and then she paused to count her stitches under her breath, and then the clicking began again.

And curled up on the hearthrug, as close to the fire as it was possible to be without actually being in it, lay a large grey cat. Its fur was soft and thick, the colour of rain clouds, and its paws were tucked neatly underneath it. As Mara sat down in the armchair nearest the fire, the cat opened one golden eye, considered her for a long moment, and closed it again. A slow, deep purr began somewhere inside it, like a very small engine idling.

"That's Biscuit," said Mrs Penrose, coming in with a tray. "He thinks the fire belongs to him. We let him go on thinking it."

On the tray there was a bowl of soup, a thick slice of bread, and a little dish of butter. The soup was made of leeks and potatoes, pale and creamy, with a swirl of cream on top and a scattering of pepper. The bread was still warm, with a dark crust that crackled when she broke it, and a soft white middle. Mara spread the butter on it and watched it melt.

She tore off one corner of the bread and dipped it in the soup, and watched the butter soften into it, until it was gone. The soup was warm all the way down, and the bread was the kind of bread that makes you close your eyes for a moment when you taste it. Outside, the rain kept up its gentle drumming on the roof. Inside, the fire crackled, and the needles clicked, and the cat purred, and the old man tied his knots, one after another.

When she had finished, Mrs Penrose took the tray away and came back with a mug of hot cocoa, and set it on the little table at Mara's elbow. Then she sat down in the chair across from her, with a sigh of someone who had been on her feet all day and was glad to be off them.

"So," she said. "What brings you all the way out to Saltmere Head, in weather like this?"

Mara told her about the maps. About walking the coast path from village to village, drawing every bend and every bench, every spring where you could fill a water bottle, every place where the view opened up and made you stop. Mrs Penrose listened, nodding, her hands folded in her lap.

"Then you'll want to know about the lighthouse," she said, when Mara had finished. "Every good map has a story or two on it."

And she told her.

The lighthouse had stood on the headland for nearly two hundred years. For most of that time, there had been a keeper living in the cottage. Every evening, the keeper climbed the tower to light the lamp, and every morning, climbed it again to put it out. In between, there was the great glass lens to polish, and the wicks to trim. And all through the long nights, winter and summer, there was the clockwork to wind, to keep the light turning. The keepers' families had lived here too. Children had grown up in these rooms, and played on these flagstones, and fallen asleep to the sound of the sea.

These days, the light mostly looked after itself. But there was still a keeper of sorts, a man called Tom, who lived in the little room at the bottom of the tower and made sure the old light kept its patient watch. He came over to the inn most evenings for his supper, Mrs Penrose said, and he would probably be along soon. And if Mara asked him nicely, he might take her up to see the lamp.

Mara wrapped both hands around her mug of cocoa, and felt the warmth of it soak into her fingers. She looked into the fire. She watched the flames curl around the logs, blue at the bottom, orange in the middle, and yellow at the tips, always moving, never the same shape twice. The heat of it rested on her face and on her knees like a blanket.

She thought she might like to see the lamp very much.

The old man by the window had finished one corner of his net, and moved on to the next. The woman in the green cardigan had turned her row, and started back along the other way. Biscuit stretched one front paw out towards the fire, spread his toes, and drew it slowly back in again. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, softly, softly.

And outside, the light went round. And round. And round.
